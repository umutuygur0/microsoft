# Corrosion Inspection

## Overview
Corrosion is the single largest long-term threat to the integrity of gas
field pipework, pressure vessels, and structural steel. Unlike a sudden
mechanical failure, corrosion usually progresses slowly and invisibly under
insulation, coatings, or simply on an internal surface no one looks at day
to day — which is exactly why a routine, scheduled inspection programme
matters more than reacting to visible damage. The goal of this procedure is
to detect and assess corrosion before wall loss threatens the pressure
integrity of the equipment, giving engineering time to plan a repair on a
controlled schedule rather than in response to an emergency.

## Key Safety Precautions
- Corroded components may be significantly weaker than they appear from the
  outside; do not apply load, pressure, or mechanical force to any item
  identified or suspected as corroded until it has been formally assessed.
- Wear appropriate PPE when removing insulation, since corrosion under
  insulation (CUI) is frequently hidden beneath material that must be
  disturbed to inspect the surface beneath.
- Removing insulation or coating can locally reduce the strength of an
  already-thinned component; treat every insulation removal as a task that
  itself needs a brief risk assessment, not just routine housekeeping.

## Inspection Methods
No single method catches every form of corrosion, which is why inspection
programmes typically combine several techniques:
- **Visual inspection** for pitting, blistering, staining, and surface rust
  is the fastest method and the first line of defence, but it only detects
  corrosion that has already broken through a coating or is otherwise
  externally visible.
- **Ultrasonic thickness (UT) measurement** checks the remaining wall
  thickness directly through the material, and is the primary method for
  quantifying general wall loss and comparing it against the minimum
  allowable thickness calculated by engineering.
- **Removal of insulation at sample points** to check for corrosion under
  insulation (CUI), which is one of the most common causes of unexpected
  wall loss precisely because it is invisible during a routine walk-down.

## Working Procedure
1. Clean the surface to bare metal at each measurement point — readings
   taken through paint, scale, or residue are unreliable and can mask real
   wall loss.
2. Record wall thickness readings and compare them against the minimum
   allowable value for that component, as defined by the engineering
   integrity assessment.
3. Photograph and map any pitting or general wall loss, recording its
   location precisely enough that the same spot can be re-measured on the
   next inspection to establish a corrosion rate.
4. Flag any reading below the minimum allowable value for immediate
   engineering assessment — do not wait for the next scheduled inspection
   cycle to raise a below-minimum finding.
5. Reinstate coatings and insulation after inspection, since leaving a
   surface exposed after inspection can accelerate the very corrosion the
   inspection was meant to catch.
6. Update the corrosion monitoring records and trend the data over time —
   a single reading tells you the current state, but a trend tells you how
   fast the equipment is degrading and when it will need intervention.

## Interpreting Results
A reading close to, but still above, the minimum allowable thickness is not
automatically a "pass" if the trend shows rapid ongoing loss. Two components
with identical current readings can have very different urgency depending on
their corrosion rate: one that has lost 0.5 mm over ten years is a very
different case from one that has lost 0.5 mm in the last year. Engineering
should always be given both the absolute reading and the trend, not the
reading alone.
