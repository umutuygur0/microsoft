# Gas Detector Calibration

## Overview
A gas detector is only as trustworthy as its last successful calibration.
Sensors drift over time due to sensor ageing, poisoning by certain
chemicals, or simple wear, and a detector that has silently drifted
out of specification gives a false sense of security that is arguably worse
than having no detector at all — a worker relying on a reading of "zero" from
a detector that can no longer actually detect gas is walking into a hazard
unaware. Regular bump testing and calibration exist to catch that drift
before it matters.

## Key Safety Precautions
- Use only certified calibration gas that is within its expiry date;
  expired calibration gas can itself have drifted in concentration and will
  produce a false calibration.
- Calibrate in a well-ventilated area away from ignition sources, since the
  process necessarily involves releasing a small amount of test gas.
- A detector that fails calibration must be removed from service
  immediately — it must not be returned to a worker "until we get a chance
  to look at it properly," since that worker may rely on it in the interim.

## Definitions
- **Bump test:** a brief, deliberate exposure of the detector to test gas to
  confirm the sensor responds and the audible/visual alarms activate. A bump
  test does not adjust the reading — it only confirms the detector is
  capable of responding at all.
- **Calibration:** the process of adjusting the detector's reading so that
  it matches a known gas concentration exactly, correcting for any drift
  that has occurred since the last calibration.

## Working Procedure
1. Confirm the calibration gas type and concentration match the sensor
   being calibrated — using the wrong gas type produces a meaningless result
   even if the process is followed correctly in every other respect.
2. Perform a fresh-air zero in clean, known-good air before applying any
   test gas, so that the zero point is established correctly.
3. Apply the calibration gas at the specified flow rate using the correct
   regulator and hose set for that gas type.
4. Allow the reading to stabilise fully, then adjust the span setting to
   match the certified concentration of the calibration gas.
5. Confirm the low and high alarms activate at their correct set points
   during the same session — a detector that reads accurately but whose
   alarms do not trigger correctly is still unsafe to deploy.
6. Record the calibration date, the calibration gas lot number, and the
   result in the equipment register, so that the detector's history is
   traceable.
7. Apply a calibration due sticker to the physical unit and update the
   equipment register so the next due date is visible to anyone checking
   the device out.

## Frequency
- **Bump test:** before each day of use — this is a quick check, not a full
  calibration, and should never be skipped even when time is tight.
- **Full calibration:** at least every 6 months, or more frequently per
  manufacturer guidance or if a bump test result is borderline.

## When a Detector Fails a Bump Test
A failed bump test does not necessarily mean the detector is broken — it
could indicate a depleted sensor, a blocked inlet, or a battery issue — but
it does mean the detector cannot be trusted until the underlying cause is
identified. The correct response is always the same regardless of suspected
cause: remove from service, tag it clearly, and route it to a full
calibration or repair before it is issued again.
