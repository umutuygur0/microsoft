# Confined Space Entry

## Overview
A confined space — a separator vessel, storage tank, pit, or similar
enclosure — is dangerous precisely because it is not designed for continuous
human occupancy: ventilation is poor, access and egress are restricted, and
the atmosphere inside can be completely different from the atmosphere
outside the entry point. More fatalities in industrial settings occur among
would-be rescuers than among the original casualty, because the same
atmosphere that overcame the first person also overcomes anyone who enters
without protection to help them. This procedure exists to make sure entry
only happens when the atmosphere and the rescue arrangements have both been
verified, every single time.

## Key Safety Precautions
- Never enter a confined space without a valid entry permit and a completed
  gas test — "it was fine yesterday" is not a substitute for testing today.
- A trained standby attendant must remain outside for the entire duration of
  the entry, in constant communication with those inside and never leaving
  their post to perform another task.
- Atmospheres inside a confined space can change rapidly — a purge that
  displaces oxygen, a residual pocket of gas disturbed by movement, or a
  process upset elsewhere in the plant can turn a safe space dangerous
  within minutes. Continuous monitoring, not a single test at the start, is
  mandatory.

## Atmospheric Test Limits
- Oxygen: 19.5% to 23.5%. Below 19.5% the atmosphere is oxygen-deficient;
  above 23.5% it is oxygen-enriched and significantly increases fire risk.
- Combustible gas: below 10% of the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL).
- Hydrogen sulphide (H2S): below 5 ppm.
- Carbon monoxide: below 25 ppm.

These limits are tested at multiple heights because gases do not distribute
evenly — H2S and other heavier-than-air gases tend to pool at the bottom of a
vessel, while some lighter gases accumulate near the top.

## Working Procedure
1. Isolate, drain, purge, and — where required — ventilate the space before
   any testing begins.
2. Complete atmospheric testing at top, middle, and bottom levels of the
   space, since a single mid-height reading can miss a dangerous pocket
   elsewhere.
3. Issue the confined space entry permit, naming every entrant and the
   standby attendant by name — permits issued to a role rather than a named
   individual are not acceptable.
4. Set up rescue equipment (retrieval lines, tripod, breathing apparatus as
   applicable) and confirm communications before anyone enters.
5. Maintain continuous gas monitoring throughout the work, not just at entry.
6. Log entry and exit times for every person, so that at any moment it is
   known exactly who is inside.
7. Close the permit when work is complete and all personnel are confirmed
   out — a permit is not closed until the headcount matches.

## Roles and Responsibilities
- **Entrant:** completes required training, uses issued equipment correctly,
  and exits immediately if conditions change or personal alarms activate.
- **Standby attendant:** never enters the space themselves, maintains
  continuous contact with entrants, and initiates the emergency rescue
  procedure without entering if contact is lost.
- **Permit issuer:** verifies isolation, testing, and rescue arrangements are
  all in place before authorising entry, and has the authority to suspend
  entry at any time.
