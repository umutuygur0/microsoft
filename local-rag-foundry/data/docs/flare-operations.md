# Flare System Operation

## Overview
A flare system exists to safely dispose of gas that must be relieved from
process equipment — whether from a planned blowdown, a relief valve lift, or
an emergency depressurisation — by burning it in a controlled way rather
than releasing it unburned to atmosphere. A flare that is operating
correctly is one of the least noticed pieces of equipment on the field;
one that fails to ignite when called upon, or that loses its pilot flame
during a real relief event, turns a controlled safety system into an
uncontrolled gas release at exactly the moment it was needed most.

## Key Safety Precautions
- Never isolate the flare header while any equipment upstream can still
  relieve to it — doing so removes the only safe disposal route for that
  equipment's relief valves.
- A flame-out with continued gas flow releases unburned gas to atmosphere
  and must be treated as an emergency, not a nuisance alarm to be silenced.
- Maintain the purge gas flow at all times to prevent air ingress into the
  flare header; air mixing with hydrocarbon vapour inside the header creates
  a flashback risk that can propagate fire back toward the source equipment.

## Monitoring
- Confirm the pilot flame is lit using the pilot thermocouple reading and,
  where fitted, the flare camera — a thermocouple reading alone can
  occasionally be misleading, so cross-checking with visual confirmation
  matters during any period of doubt.
- Maintain minimum purge gas flow per the operating envelope defined for the
  flare, adjusting as ambient conditions change.
- Monitor the knock-out drum level continuously to prevent liquid
  carry-over reaching the flare tip, since burning liquid droplets can
  produce large flaming particles ("rain-out") that are a fire hazard on
  the ground below the flare.

## Working Procedure
1. Confirm the pilot flame status before and during any flaring event —
   never assume a pilot that was lit at shift start is still lit hours
   later without checking.
2. Verify the knock-out drum level is within limits and that the drain pump
   is available before a significant relief event begins.
3. Monitor flare gas flow and visible smoke, adjusting steam or air assist
   as needed to reduce smoking — persistent black smoke usually indicates
   incomplete combustion that assist air or steam is meant to correct.
4. On pilot flame-out, re-ignite immediately using the ignition system; do
   not wait to see whether the flow will self-extinguish.
5. If re-ignition fails while gas is still flowing to the flare, initiate
   the site emergency procedure without delay.
6. Log flaring volumes for environmental reporting, since flared volumes are
   typically subject to regulatory limits and reporting requirements.

## Why Flares Are Not "Just Burning Off Gas"
It is tempting to think of a flare as a simple safety valve for gas the
process does not need, but a functioning flare system is itself a critical
safety barrier: every relief valve on the site depends on the flare header
being available and correctly purged to do its job safely. Treating flare
system integrity — purge gas, pilot reliability, knock-out drum
housekeeping — with the same seriousness as any other safety-critical system
is essential, not optional.
