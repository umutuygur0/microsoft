# First Aid for Gas Exposure

## Overview
A casualty overcome by toxic or asphyxiant gas represents one of the most
dangerous rescue scenarios on a gas field, precisely because the instinct to
rush in and help is exactly the wrong first move. The same atmosphere that
overcame the original casualty will overcome an untrained rescuer within
seconds, and a significant fraction of multiple-fatality incidents in this
industry involve would-be rescuers who became casualties themselves. This
procedure exists to channel that instinct to help into actions that actually
save lives rather than add to the casualty count.

## Key Safety Precautions
- Do NOT enter the contaminated area to attempt a rescue without breathing
  apparatus and a trained team; this is the single most important rule in
  this procedure and overrides the urge to act immediately.
- Assume the atmosphere is still hazardous until it has been confirmed clear
  by gas testing — a casualty who has stopped moving does not mean the gas
  has dispersed.

## Working Procedure
1. Raise the alarm and call for the emergency response team immediately;
   do not delay this step to attempt anything else first.
2. Only a team equipped with breathing apparatus should enter to remove the
   casualty, bringing them to fresh air, upwind of the source.
3. Check for breathing once the casualty is in a safe location; if breathing
   is absent, begin CPR immediately if trained to do so.
4. If the casualty is breathing, place them in the recovery position and
   keep their airway clear of obstruction.
5. Administer oxygen if trained to do so and equipment is available on site.
6. Keep the casualty warm and continue monitoring their condition until
   medical help arrives — a casualty who appears to be recovering can still
   deteriorate.
7. Record the gas involved and the approximate exposure time for the
   attending medical staff; this information materially changes how a
   casualty is treated.

## H2S Specific
Hydrogen sulphide deserves particular caution because it deadens the sense
of smell at dangerous concentrations, meaning a casualty (and potentially a
rescuer) may have no warning that the gas is present at all. H2S exposure
can also cause delayed lung injury that is not apparent immediately after
exposure — for this reason, all persons exposed to H2S must be medically
assessed even if they appear to have fully recovered at the scene.

## Recognising Exposure
Symptoms of toxic or asphyxiant gas exposure can include confusion,
loss of coordination, sudden collapse, or — in the case of asphyxiants
that simply displace oxygen — no warning symptoms at all before
unconsciousness. Anyone showing these signs in or near a known gas hazard
area should be treated as a potential gas exposure casualty even if the
cause is not immediately obvious.
