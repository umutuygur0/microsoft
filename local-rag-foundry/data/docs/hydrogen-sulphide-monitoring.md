# Hydrogen Sulphide (H2S) Monitoring

## Overview
Hydrogen sulphide is a highly toxic gas naturally present in "sour" gas
fields, and it is unusual among industrial hazards in that the danger
increases as the warning signs disappear: at low concentrations H2S has a
distinctive rotten-egg smell, but at higher, more dangerous concentrations
it rapidly deadens the sense of smell, so a worker may believe the gas has
gone away at the exact moment exposure is becoming lethal. This procedure
describes the monitoring and response measures that exist because human
senses cannot be relied on to provide adequate warning.

## Key Safety Precautions
- H2S is toxic and can be fatal within minutes at high concentrations —
  unlike many hazards, there is very little margin between "detectable" and
  "dangerous" once concentrations rise.
- Do not rely on smell as an indicator of safety; H2S deadens the sense of
  smell at dangerous levels, meaning the absence of odour is not evidence of
  the absence of gas.
- If a high alarm sounds, hold your breath, leave the area immediately in
  an upwind direction, and raise the alarm — do not pause to investigate the
  source first.

## Alarm Set Points
- **Low alarm: 5 ppm.** This threshold is set well below any acute health
  effect, giving time to investigate and correct the source before exposure
  becomes hazardous.
- **High alarm: 10 ppm.** At this level, immediate evacuation is required;
  this is not a threshold for cautious continuation of work.
- **Immediately Dangerous to Life or Health (IDLH): 100 ppm.** At or above
  this concentration, unprotected exposure can cause rapid loss of
  consciousness and death; this level must never be approached without
  breathing apparatus.

## Working Procedure
1. Wear a bump-tested personal H2S monitor at all times in sour-gas areas —
   personal monitors exist precisely because fixed detectors cannot cover
   every location a worker might move through.
2. Confirm fixed detector readings before entering a work area, treating a
   silent or offline fixed detector as reason for extra caution, not as
   reassurance.
3. On a low alarm, stop work and assess the source before continuing —
   a low alarm that is ignored and allowed to recur is how a low alarm
   becomes a high alarm.
4. On a high alarm, evacuate upwind to the muster point immediately, without
   attempting to identify or fix the source first.
5. Do not attempt a rescue without breathing apparatus and a trained team,
   even for a colleague who has collapsed — see the First Aid for Gas
   Exposure procedure for the full rescue protocol.
6. Report all alarms, even brief or seemingly spurious ones, to the shift
   supervisor; a pattern of brief alarms over time can indicate a
   developing leak that has not yet become continuous.

## Why H2S Is Treated Differently From Combustible Gas
Combustible gas detection is primarily concerned with explosion risk, and a
reading well below the Lower Explosive Limit is generally considered safe to
continue working in. H2S monitoring has no equivalent comfortable margin —
health effects begin at concentrations far below anything relevant to
explosion risk, which is why H2S alarm thresholds are set in single-digit
parts per million rather than as a percentage of an explosive limit.
