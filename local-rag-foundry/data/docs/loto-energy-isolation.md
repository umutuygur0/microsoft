# Lockout / Tagout (LOTO)

## Overview
Lockout/tagout exists to guarantee that equipment being maintained cannot be
re-energised — accidentally, remotely, or by someone unaware that work is
underway — while a person's hands, or body, may be inside or on it. Verbal
agreements ("just don't start it, I'm working on it") fail exactly when they
matter most: during shift changes, when multiple trades are working on the
same equipment, or under the pressure of a schedule. A physical lock,
applied and removed only by the person who applied it, removes that
dependence on memory and communication.

## Key Safety Precautions
- Each person working on the equipment must apply their own personal lock —
  a single lock applied by a supervisor on behalf of a work crew does not
  protect every individual, since one person leaving does not tell the
  others it is now unsafe to remove that lock.
- Never remove another worker's lock or tag, even if you believe their work
  is finished; only the person who applied a lock can be certain it is safe
  to remove it.
- Verify a zero energy state before starting work, and always assume
  equipment is energised until this has been proven — this default
  assumption, rather than trusting a label or a switch position, is what
  actually protects against errors elsewhere in the system.

## Energy Sources to Isolate
Equipment can hold hazardous energy in more forms than the obvious main
power switch suggests, and a LOTO that isolates only the most visible source
is incomplete:
- Process pressure, both gas and liquid, which can remain trapped in a
  section even after upstream and downstream valves are closed.
- Electrical supply to motors and actuators, including control circuits
  that may operate at a different voltage or source than the main power feed.
- Stored mechanical energy such as springs, counterweights, or raised loads,
  which can move without any external power at all.
- Instrument air and hydraulic pressure, which can hold valves or actuators
  in position and release that position suddenly if bled uncontrolled.

## Working Procedure
1. Identify all energy sources feeding the equipment, using the equipment's
   isolation drawing rather than relying on memory or assumption.
2. Shut down the equipment using its normal stopping procedure before
   attempting isolation.
3. Isolate each energy source in turn and apply a personal lock and tag to
   each isolation point.
4. Release or block any stored energy — bleed trapped pressure, discharge
   capacitors, block raised components — since isolating the source of new
   energy does not remove energy already stored in the system.
5. Verify isolation by attempting a controlled start (a "try-out"); the
   equipment must not run. This step confirms the isolation is real, not
   just assumed.
6. Perform the maintenance work, with each worker's lock remaining in place
   for the entire duration of their involvement.
7. Remove locks only in reverse order, and only once the area is confirmed
   clear of personnel and tools.

## Multiple-Lock Situations
When several people or trades work on the same equipment, a lock box or
group lockout device is used: each individual's personal lock secures the
box, and the equipment cannot be released until every lock has been removed
by its owner. This preserves the "my lock, my safety" principle even when
many people are involved simultaneously.
