# Pipeline Pressure Testing

## Overview
Before a new or repaired pipeline section is returned to service, it must be
proven to hold pressure safely under conditions more demanding than normal
operation. Hydrostatic and pneumatic pressure testing exist to catch
defects — a missed weld flaw, a damaged fitting, a material defect — while
the section is isolated and prepared for testing, rather than discovering
them after the line is carrying live product and surrounded by normal site
activity.

## Key Safety Precautions
- Pneumatic testing stores a very large amount of energy in compressed gas,
  far more than an equivalent hydrostatic test using nearly incompressible
  water; keep all personnel outside the calculated exclusion radius during
  pressurisation, since a sudden failure releases that stored energy almost
  instantaneously.
- Never exceed the maximum allowable test pressure stated on the pipeline
  data sheet — typically 1.5 times the design pressure for hydrostatic
  tests — since testing beyond this margin risks damaging sound pipe rather
  than only revealing genuine defects.
- Depressurise slowly at the end of the test; a rapid release can damage
  seals, shock the pipe material, and injure personnel working nearby.

## Test Parameters
- **Hydrostatic test medium:** clean water with a corrosion inhibitor added,
  chosen because water's near-incompressibility means a failure releases far
  less stored energy than an equivalent gas test.
- **Standard hold time:** 4 hours minimum after the test medium's
  temperature has stabilised, since temperature changes alone can produce a
  pressure change that would otherwise be mistaken for a leak.
- **Acceptable pressure drop:** less than 0.5% of test pressure over the
  hold period — anything beyond this is treated as an indication of a leak
  requiring investigation, not measurement noise.

## Working Procedure
1. Isolate and blank the test section, and verify every valve associated
   with the section is in its correct position before introducing any test
   medium.
2. Fill the section with test medium, venting air from the highest point —
   trapped air compresses and expands with temperature far more than the
   liquid does, which can produce misleading pressure readings during the
   hold period if not removed.
3. Raise pressure in 25% increments, holding for 10 minutes at each step to
   inspect for leaks before continuing to the next increment.
4. Reach the target test pressure and begin the timed hold once temperature
   has stabilised.
5. Record pressure and temperature every 15 minutes throughout the hold,
   since both readings are needed to distinguish a genuine pressure drop
   from a drop caused by cooling.
6. On successful completion, depressurise gradually and drain the test
   medium to an approved disposal point.
7. Complete the test certificate and attach the pressure chart, since this
   record is the formal evidence that the section is fit to return to
   service.

## Why Staged Pressurisation Matters
Raising pressure in increments rather than all at once gives the test crew
repeated opportunities to catch a developing leak or a weak point before it
reaches full test pressure, when a failure would release the most energy. A
defect that shows itself as a slow weep at 50% of test pressure is a
straightforward repair; the same defect reached without staged inspection,
at full test pressure, is a very different and more hazardous event.
