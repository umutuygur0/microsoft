# Valve Inspection and Maintenance

## Overview
Isolation and control valves are the components a gas field relies on most
directly for safety, since nearly every emergency and maintenance procedure
in this knowledge base ultimately depends on a valve closing reliably and
sealing tightly when it is needed. A valve that has not been exercised or
lubricated regularly can seize in position, fail to seal fully, or take
excessive force to operate at exactly the moment it is relied upon during an
emergency shutdown or maintenance isolation. Routine inspection and
lubrication exist to keep that reliability from degrading silently between
the moments a valve is actually called upon.

## Key Safety Precautions
- Confirm the valve is isolated and depressurised before any hands-on work
  — a valve under pressure can eject packing or grease under significant
  force if disturbed.
- Follow the lockout/tagout (LOTO) procedure and verify a zero energy state
  before beginning work, treating the valve and its actuator as
  energised until proven otherwise.
- Some valve greases are injected under pressure by the lubrication system;
  wear eye protection when using a grease gun, since back-pressure through
  the fitting can force grease out unexpectedly.

## Inspection Steps
1. Check the valve body and bonnet for external corrosion or signs of
   leakage, since these are often the first visible indication of an
   internal seal degrading.
2. Operate the valve through a full open-close cycle, noting any unusual
   stiffness or binding that could indicate internal wear or debris.
3. Verify the position indicator matches the actual valve state — a
   mismatched indicator can mislead an operator into believing a valve is
   open or closed when it is not, with serious consequences if that valve
   is relied upon during an emergency.
4. Inspect the stem seal and gland packing for signs of weeping, which
   indicates the primary seal against the process pressure is beginning to
   degrade.
5. Check actuator air supply pressure for pneumatic actuators, which should
   read between 5.5 and 7 bar — pressure outside this range can mean the
   actuator does not have enough force to fully open or close the valve
   when commanded.

## Lubrication
- Inject the specified valve grease through the sealant fitting until back
  pressure is felt, which indicates the grease has fully filled the
  sealant chamber around the valve seat.
- Do not over-inject; excessive sealant pressure can actually jam the ball
  or gate in position rather than freeing it, defeating the purpose of the
  lubrication.
- Record the grease type, quantity used, and date on the maintenance log,
  since this record helps identify a valve that is consuming grease faster
  than expected — often an early sign of a developing seal problem.

## Why Position Indication Matters as Much as the Valve Itself
A valve that operates correctly but reports its position incorrectly is, in
practical terms, just as dangerous as a valve that has genuinely seized —
in both cases, an operator relying on the indicated position to make a
safety decision is working from false information. Position indicator
checks are therefore never a formality to skip when time is short; they are
as safety-critical as the mechanical inspection of the valve itself.
