# Cold Weather Operations

## Overview
Freezing conditions change how a gas field behaves. Water that is normally
carried harmlessly through a pipeline can combine with natural gas under
pressure to form hydrates — ice-like solids that can block a line as
effectively as a wax plug. Instrumentation and impulse lines that read fine
in summer can freeze solid in winter, giving false or frozen readings right
when operators need them most. This procedure sets out the precautions needed
to keep equipment running and personnel safe when temperatures fall below
freezing for extended periods.

## Key Safety Precautions
- Ice can hide leaks and block relief paths; inspect vents and reliefs for
  ice build-up before assuming a system is operating normally.
- Frozen ground and icy walkways significantly increase slip hazards and
  vehicle stopping distances — adjust site speed limits and pedestrian routes
  accordingly during freeze events.
- Metal surfaces at ambient cold-weather temperatures can cause cold burns to
  bare skin in seconds; wear insulated gloves for any contact with exposed
  metalwork.
- Cold-soaked steel becomes more brittle; impact loads (dropped tools,
  sudden valve operation) are more likely to cause cracking than in warm
  conditions.

## Hydrate and Freeze Prevention
Hydrates form when free water, natural gas, and the right combination of
pressure and temperature come together — conditions that are common at
choke points, pressure-reducing valves, and anywhere gas expands and cools.
Prevention relies on keeping water out of the gas stream or keeping the gas
warm enough, or dry enough, that hydrates cannot form in the first place:
- Maintain methanol or glycol injection at the rate specified for the current
  ambient temperature; injection rates are not fixed and should be reviewed
  as temperatures drop further.
- Keep instrument and impulse lines heat-traced and insulated, and confirm
  the heat-tracing circuit breakers have not tripped during routine rounds.
- Drain low points in piping where water can accumulate and freeze, since a
  frozen low point can block flow even where the main line remains clear.

## Working Procedure
1. Confirm heat tracing and insulation are energised and physically intact —
   damaged insulation is often invisible until a line has already frozen.
2. Check methanol or glycol injection rates and tank levels; a nearly empty
   inhibitor tank in the middle of a cold snap is a leading indicator of an
   imminent hydrate problem, not just a housekeeping issue.
3. Inspect vents, drains, and relief valves for ice blockage — a relief valve
   that cannot lift because its outlet is iced shut provides no overpressure
   protection at all.
4. Monitor for unexplained pressure drops across chokes or valves, which can
   indicate a hydrate plug forming before the line fully blocks.
5. Never apply direct flame to thaw frozen equipment; use approved heating
   methods such as hot water jackets, heat tape, or warm-air blowers — a
   naked flame near a hydrocarbon system is a fire and explosion risk
   regardless of how cold the equipment feels.
6. Increase inspection frequency during extreme cold or rapidly falling
   temperatures, since freeze-related failures tend to develop faster than
   the routine inspection schedule assumes.
